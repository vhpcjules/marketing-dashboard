Initiative 1  Completed · July 1 – July 31

Instagram giveaway — final results, and what happened after

The month-long YETI cooler + drinkware giveaway wrapped July 31. Final Insights numbers reconciled against Supermetrics — this was the strongest single organic asset on the channel this year and drove more follower growth than any single post in memory.

The campaign at a glance

Target: Current + prospective Instagram followers, and their networks (via UGC from contractor collab posts).
Offer: Red YETI Roadie cooler + white YETI water bottle + navy YETI tumbler (all flag-branded).
Window: July 1 – July 31.
Boost: $40 boosted across Meta — drove 176 of the 203 profile visits at $0.23 per visit.

Why this is a real win: The June baseline for the whole Instagram channel was 28 new followers for the entire month (per Social Media dashboard). This single giveaway post drove 155 new followers on its own — 5.5× the monthly baseline in one asset. The 76% follow-through rate from profile visits (155 of 203) is well above what's typical for a giveaway — meaning the audience arriving is real and interested, not just prize-hunters. And 375 interactions on 4,134 views is a 9.1% engagement rate, which is exceptional for the channel's scale.
The bigger story — earned amplification from contractors: Two contractors created their own posts tagging VHPC, and both are performing well organically on their own feeds. This is the outcome that actually matters more than the direct metrics. Contractor-generated content in the wild is what turns a giveaway from "a nice traffic bump" into "brand credibility inside the community we're trying to sell to." Recommend we reach out to both contractors, thank them personally, and open a conversation about a lightweight ongoing collab or affiliate arrangement.

Three weeks later — the followers are unwinding

The +155 is not holding. Instagram followers peaked at 7,015 on July 31 and sit at 6,903 as of August 18 — a net loss of 112, which is more than the giveaway gained. Giveaway follower churn is normal and expected, but it means the +155 should not be carried forward as durable audience growth. The honest framing for leadership: the giveaway bought a spike in attention, not a permanent audience.

What did hold: profile views. 29,755 in July, and 11,929 in the first 18 days of August — tracking close to July's pace even without an active promotion. And the two contractor UGC posts are still earning on their own feeds. Attention and credibility persisted; the follower count did not.
Recommendation — stop running giveaways for follower growth. Two data points now say the same thing. The giveaway added 155 followers and gave back 112. Meanwhile the pickleball case-study post — a real customer install with product specs and clean photos, costing nothing but time — was the top performer on both Instagram and LinkedIn. Case-study content earns an audience that stays. If we run another giveaway, the goal should be UGC and email capture, not follower count.

The August audience update is working — and the read is available now. The new third-party lookalike is producing leads at $25.03 versus the original targeting's $22.32. That is parity, on a fifth of the spend, in its first few weeks. Frequency is slightly lower too (2.03 vs 2.22), which means it has more room to run before fatigue. Why $22.80 matters against our own numbers. Blended cost per customer is $524. The CPL in that recent window is actually better, under $10, so this is not a performance problem; it is an underspend. If delivery is being constrained by audience size or a bid cap, the best-performing acquisition asset we have is being held back. Worth 15 minutes in Ads Manager.
Initiative 3  Live · 2 of 4 flows reporting

Email nurture flows in NetSuite — first real results, after a targeting fix

Four automated lifecycle sequences live in NetSuite. Two are now reporting. The first month did not work as designed, for a reason worth understanding.

What went wrong, and what fixed it. The new-lead sequence (no purchase) was built on criteria that turned out to be too narrow. It could not see newsletter subscribers, phone-in leads, or leads imported from Meta — which is a large share of the people it was designed to reach. The campaigns also only fire when a new person enters the list, so nobody already sitting in it got picked up. A manual push was required to capture the existing list. Criteria are now corrected and both reporting campaigns are delivering.

Why this connects to something bigger: the leads this flow could not see are the same population sitting unassigned in NetSuite — 104 of July's 278 records have no sales rep, are email-only, and convert at 1%. Same root cause, two symptoms. The nurture fix addresses the marketing half; the routing automation still needs work.

The ask  Decision needed · September

New-customer retargeting on Google — $1,000/month, September start

A new Google retargeting campaign that follows every new customer from their first purchase, with one job: get the second order. This is incremental spend, not a reallocation — Meta budget stays where it is. Below is what the data says about whether the window and the audience are right.

The campaign

Audience: New customers, entered on first purchase. A second purchase disqualifies and exits them.
Window: 90 days of follow.
Goal: Accelerate the second order and convert one-timers into repeat buyers.
Ask: $1,000/month of new Google spend, starting September.
Rationale: Stay in front of new customers rather than letting them drift to another supplier.

What the data says about the design

Median time to second order: 17 days. 51% of everyone who ever reorders has done so by day 17, 63% by day 30, 83% by day 90.

The 90-day window is the right length — it captures 83% of natural repeat behaviour. Only 17% of repeaters convert after day 90, so extending further has sharply diminishing returns.

Two refinements the data supports

RefinementEvidenceWhy it matters
Target small first orders hardest Reorder rate by first-order size: under $400 → 35.4%; $400–2,499 → 49.1%; over $2,500 → 50.9% Sample-scale buyers are 28% less likely to return (35.4% vs 49.1%). There are 591 one-and-done buyers in this band. Weighting spend here targets the actual leak rather than spreading evenly across new customers.
Front-load the first 30 days 63% of eventual second orders happen by day 30; only 20% land between day 30 and day 90 Spend should be heaviest in the first two to three weeks, when the decision is actually being made, then taper. A flat 90-day spend curve wastes budget on a period where little converts.
Why this is worth $1,000 of new money. The weakness it targets is the clearest one in the business: roughly two-thirds of every cohort never places a second order, and the 2025 cohort's 21.5% repeat rate happened passively — nobody was asking them to come back. At the current average of about 62 new customers a month, $1,000 is roughly $16 per new customer for a 90-day follow. Set against a $524 blended cost to acquire each of them and a $1,059 average first order, that is a small premium to protect an asset we have already paid for. The Google-side lookalike built in August and the TRU first-party data work both make this campaign land better than it would have three months ago.
One thing we still cannot answer: why qualified customers don't come back. The transaction data shows the what — a 35.4% reorder rate on small first orders, a 17-day median, 591 one-and-done buyers — but not the why. Retargeting treats the symptom by staying visible. It will not tell us whether people leave over price, product confusion, or a bad first experience. The second focus group is the cheaper instrument for that question — $1,750 one-time against $3,000 for the 90-day retargeting run — and the two should run together: retargeting to hold the customers, research to find the cause.

How to judge it: the honest success measure is not clicks or impressions — it is whether the median time to second order moves below 17 days, and whether the reorder rate for sub-$400 first orders climbs off 35.4%. Both are measurable in NetSuite without any attribution wiring, by comparing September-onward cohorts against the pre-campaign baseline.
Initiative 5  In flight · content is time-only Focus group 2 · $1,750 ask

Search and AI visibility review — plus customer research

The content work needs time rather than spend. The research that surfaced the gap does carry a cost — $1,750 per focus group. Recent primary research turned this from a hypothesis into a documented gap.

Customers are asking AI assistants the exact questions we have no coverage for. In the August focus group, a 20-year customer described using ChatGPT to decide which product to buy — specifically asking whether a spec difference "3 versus 9" mattered for his job, because our own materials didn't tell him. His words: "I wish they would just tell me that. Does this product work for something that's going to have forklifts on it?"

We had already measured the other side of this: 0% visibility on every failure-mode prompt tested in HubSpot's AEO tool, and zero Search Console impressions on failure-mode queries. Demand exists, we are invisible for it, and customers are getting answers about our products from somewhere that isn't us. These were 55–60+ year-old veterans — the least likely group to be doing this.
The plan
Failure-mode content
Build out informative blog and spec content targeting the job questions customers actually ask — forklift traffic, moisture, chip resistance, product selection by environment. Costs time, not budget. Jon to review and validate technical accuracy.
Measured demand already there
230K impressions
The polyaspartic family already earns 230,000 Search Console impressions at an average position of 8. Product numbers rank on their own. The gap is not awareness — it is that we publish nothing for the failure-mode and selection questions.
Content the customers asked for
Real-garage video
Focus group was specific: our videos are all shot in a studio. "We want to see this in a real garage... how they're actually prepping the floor." Floor prep named directly. One production effort serves both the video gap and the AI-answer gap.
Focus group 2 — new customers
$1,750
Per group, one-time — Current Customer Focus Group. Group 1 was three 20-year veterans buying on habit — strong on trust and friction, weak on how new buyers decide. Group 2 targets customers under two years, focused on journey: job-driven vs price-driven, where they self-educate, and whether they run competitor products alongside ours. This is the only instrument on this page that answers why two-thirds of a cohort never reorders.
What group 1 told us that changes the product work: tier names built on customer identity do not work, because every contractor is simultaneously a "pro" and a "core" customer — the job changes, not the person. Their repeated ask was to name the job, not the buyer. "Specialty" and "Elite" landed; "Pro," "Core," "Rogue," "Signature" and "Select" did not. None of the three knew we run two product lines, and all three primarily phone in orders because the website has, in their words, "too many things — I don't even know where to start."
